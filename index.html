Full summary background and the full wedge description

Software-defined vehicles are sold with the expectation that they will keep improving through software updates. However, vehicles stay in service for well over a decade, while the computers and cellular modems inside them become outdated much sooner. When a cellular network shuts down or a carmaker ends software support, connected features stop working and security updates stop arriving. This already happened at scale when 3G networks shut down in 2022,2 and industry coverage has raised the same concern for 4G.4

The carmaker decides how long a vehicle receives software support, but it no longer owns the vehicle after the first sale. Fleets, later owners, repair shops, and used-car buyers absorb the consequences, and they have little visibility into when support or connectivity will end.

Lead wedge (W1): a software and connectivity audit for public and utility fleets, built for the shop supervisor or telematics technician who keeps the fleet's connected systems working. They provide a list of vehicle identification numbers (VINs) and receive, for each vehicle, the connected hardware it has, the network it depends on, when service or software support ends, and the recommended fix: a software update, a module replacement, an add-on retrofit, or replacing the vehicle. The fleet manager uses the same report to budget.

Longer term: if W1 proves useful, the same database could support a software condition report for used vehicles (W2), which is a larger market.

Full problem statement the detailed situation, with sources
Who has the problem
The fleet shop supervisor or telematics technician who keeps a public or utility fleet's connected systems working, at places such as a city street department, a county highway department, or a school district transportation office. The fleet manager, who approves the budget, feels the cost second.
Their situation
Fleet vehicles contain factory-installed cellular modems and software that depend on cellular networks and on carmaker support. When a connected feature such as remote diagnostics, location tracking, or automatic crash notification stops working, the technician has to work out, one vehicle at a time, which modem and telematics hardware is installed, which network it uses, whether the cause is a network shutdown, an end of software support, or a hardware fault, and whether a software update, a replacement module, or an add-on device will fix it. That information is spread across build specifications, owner manuals, and service bulletins that vary by make, model, and year, and we have not found a tool that ties it to the VIN. The 3G shutdown in 2022 showed that this can affect many vehicles at once.23
What would be better
If the technician could see in advance, by VIN, what connected hardware each vehicle has, when it loses service, and what the fix is, the shop could schedule retrofits during routine maintenance and give the fleet manager a costed list for the budget, instead of diagnosing failures after features stop working.

This statement is based on public sources. We have not yet confirmed it with fleet technicians or managers; those interviews are the first step in our test plan.

Decision trail · Scorecard: Process

How we chose this focus area

We rejected two earlier ideas before choosing the vehicle after the sale.

Ideas we considered and why we changed direction five decisions
RejectedV2X with an emergency stop device

The stopping function already exists as automatic emergency braking. V2X only works once most surrounding vehicles have it, and safety-critical braking is beyond what a student team can credibly design and validate.

Changed direction"How does a software change get proven good enough to ship?"

We mapped 18 stages and found several open areas. However, the space has well-funded competitors (Applied Intuition was valued at $15 billion in 202519), the people we would need to interview work inside carmakers, and the buyer is difficult for a student team to reach. We kept the scoring method and one wedge idea, which became W3.

RejectedUpfitter integration, OTA rollout, and vehicles powering buildings

Upfitter integration fell outside the stages we had chosen at the time. OTA rollout is likely to attract many other teams. Using vehicles to power buildings moved away from software-defined vehicles toward the energy industry.

Kept for laterSoftware condition report for used vehicles

This may be the largest opportunity, but it requires reading data from inside the vehicle, which is the most difficult part. It became W2 and builds on what W1 teaches us.

ChosenThe software-defined vehicle after the sale

It has a documented precedent in the 3G shutdown, local interview access, a clear gap between who controls the software and who owns the vehicle, and a lead wedge we can test without carmaker permission.

How we scored each stage four measures and the scores for all 18 stages

We divided a vehicle's life into 18 stages. For each stage we list who participates, what success looks like, what protects current participants, and the main engineering pain points. Each stage is scored from 1 to 5 on four measures, using the same scale as SDV Explorer. A low score means a new entrant could get in, and a high score means current participants are well protected.

A low score does not necessarily mean an opportunity is valuable. A stage can be open because no one has secured it yet, or because no one is willing to pay for it. Independent repair scores low partly because shops are under financial pressure, which makes them a difficult customer. For this reason, willingness to pay is tested separately in the registry (H-05).

These scores are our first estimates. Before Check-In, each team member will score the stages independently so we can compare results and record where we disagree.

B-1.3.4 · Candidate wedge hypotheses · Scorecard: Desirability, Feasibility, Viability, Process

Three wedge hypotheses

Three wedges with three different buyers. W1 leads. W3 is the weakest.

Lead

W1 · Software and connectivity audit for public fleets

  • What it is: upload VINs and get each vehicle's connected hardware, when it loses service, and the fix
  • For: fleet technicians (users) and fleet managers (buyers)
  • Weakest assumption (H-03): a VIN plus public data is enough to know this
  • Market, our estimate: TAM about $147M to $295M a year; SAM about $38M to $76M a year
  • Test: five technician interviews and a 25-VIN check
W1 details full claim, engineering pain point, market math, and what would disprove it

We believe fleet shop supervisors and telematics technicians at public and utility fleets would use, and their fleets would pay for, a report that identifies each vehicle's connected hardware, when it will lose service or software support, and how to fix it, because today they work this out by hand one vehicle at a time and the 3G shutdown likely affected some of their vehicles.

Persona and pain
Main persona: fleet shop supervisor or telematics technician. When a connected system fails, they identify the installed hardware and work out the cause and the fix by hand, one vehicle at a time. Buyer: the fleet manager who approves the budget.
Engineering pain point
The information needed to diagnose and plan a fix, such as modem generation, network type, telematics unit part number, software support period, and available retrofit modules, is spread across build specifications, manuals, and service bulletins that vary by make, model, and year. We have not found a tool that ties it to the VIN.
Value proposition
Upload a list of VINs and receive, for each vehicle, its connected hardware, what will stop working and when, and the recommended fix: software update, module replacement, add-on retrofit, or replacement. The shop plans the work and the manager gets a costed list.
Why now
3G networks have already shut down,2 industry coverage has raised the same concern for 4G,4 and the average vehicle on the road is 12.8 years old.1 In 2018, Trimble's John Binder said some network changes "absolutely require a rip-out-and-replace."18
Weakest technical assumption (H-03): that a vehicle's modem generation and software support status can be determined from its VIN and public data alone. A second assumption (H-04) is that telematics vendors do not already provide this. Geotab's 3G guidance asked fleets to "make an inventory of all your 3G telematics devices" and, as far as we could find, did not address modems installed by the carmaker.9 A related gap: carmakers rarely publish when software support ends, and no law sets a support period for cars.32 W1 would show network shutdown dates, which carriers announce publicly, as firm dates, and software support end as a labeled estimate (H-11). NHTSA's free VIN decoder only contains what carmakers file, so trim and equipment details are often blank.35
Market size (first estimate)
Price assumption: $1 to $2 per vehicle per month ($12 to $24 per year). This is our estimate, not a sourced figure. We set it well below the $15 to $70 per month that fleets pay for full telematics,8 because this is a much smaller product.
TAM: 12.28 million US fleet vehicles7 × $12 to $24 = about $147M to $295M per year
SAM: 3.15 million government fleet vehicles7 × $12 to $24 = about $38M to $76M per year
Known gaps: the fleet counts are from 2015, utility fleets are not reported separately, and we have not yet sized a first target market such as Indiana public fleets.
How we will test it
Interview five fleet shop supervisors or technicians, starting in Indiana, and ask them to walk through their last connected-system failure and what happened in 2022. Then complete a free manual audit for one fleet and observe whether the shop uses it.
What would disprove it
Technicians already get this from dealer or telematics vendor tools, finding it takes them little time, or the fleet is not willing to pay for it.
Second

W2 · Software condition report for used vehicles

  • What it is: a CARFAX-style report on a used vehicle's software condition
  • For: used-car dealers and auctions
  • Weakest assumption (H-07): software condition can be read without carmaker cooperation
  • Market, our estimate: TAM about $100M to $199M a year
W2 details full claim, engineering pain point, market math, and test

We believe used-car dealers and auctions would pay for a report on a vehicle's software condition, because we expect it affects a vehicle's value and they cannot see it today.

Weakest technical assumption (H-07): that software condition can be read from outside the vehicle without carmaker cooperation. There are early signs this is possible: Smartcar's API covers more than 40 brands and lists Recurrent as a customer,11 and Recurrent already sells battery health reports for used EVs.10 Access is tightening on some brands: since the 2018 model year, Chrysler, Dodge, Jeep, and Ram vehicles have a secure gateway that blocks deeper aftermarket diagnostics unless the shop registers through AutoAuth.33 In the EU, the Data Act now gives users the right to access and share their vehicle's raw data.34
Persona and pain
Used car dealer or auction inspector: cannot see software or connectivity condition when pricing a vehicle.
Engineering pain point
Software version, support status, and features that no longer work are not visible during a standard inspection.
Value proposition
A CARFAX-style report for software: whether it is still supported, which features have stopped working, and how long the connection will last.
Builds on W1
The hardware and support database from W1 would form the core of this report.
Market size (first estimate)
TAM: 19.9 million US used retail sales in 20246 × $5 to $10 per report (our assumption, paid by the dealer) = about $100M to $199M per year
SAM: the share of these vehicles with connected, updatable software. Not yet sourced.
How we will test it
Interview five dealers and auction inspectors, and attempt to read software data from three different brands.
What would disprove it
Software condition does not affect prices, or the data cannot be read without carmaker cooperation.
Third

W3 · Testing updates against older hardware

  • What it is: a tool showing which older hardware versions an update affects
  • For: carmaker validation engineers
  • Weakest assumption: we can get enough information about carmaker hardware and processes
  • Market: not yet sized
W3 details full claim, engineering pain point, and why we kept it

We believe carmaker validation teams spend significant time making sure each update still works on older hardware, and that they would use a tool showing which hardware variants an update affects.

Weakest technical assumption: that we can obtain enough information about carmakers' hardware variants and validation process to design anything useful. We currently have no access path other than sponsor contacts.
Persona and pain
Carmaker validation engineer: each update must work across a growing number of older hardware variants.
Engineering pain point
Each model year adds hardware variants to the road, and every update has to be checked against them.
Why we kept it
It keeps a connection to the engineering work our team first considered and relates to our own vehicle's software.
Market size
Not yet sized. It would require the number of variants a carmaker supports, validation hours per update, and cost per hour.

Why these three, and which is weakest

  • Each has a different buyer, so disproving one does not end the project.
  • W3 is weakest: its buyer is the hardest to reach and its market is not sized.
Our full reasoning how we chose the wedges and how market sizes are calculated

Each wedge is written as a claim that could be proven false, along with the test that would decide it. Market sizes are calculated bottom-up from cited counts and from prices that we have labeled as our own assumptions.

We chose these three wedges because each targets a different buyer (fleets, used-car sellers, and carmakers) and tests a different part of the problem, so disproving one does not end the project. W1 leads because we can test it locally and without carmaker data. W3 is our weakest wedge: its buyer works deep inside carmakers, we cannot yet estimate its market size, and our only route to validation engineers is through sponsor contacts.

Full society section detail and sources for each item

This section lists the effects our wedges could have beyond the customer, along with the gaps we have not yet addressed.

Safety
When 3G networks shut down, some vehicles lost safety-related connected features.3 A vehicle that loses a feature such as automatic crash notification without warning is a risk to its occupants. W1 is intended to make these losses visible in advance. It does not change how any vehicle operates.
Security
A vehicle that no longer receives software updates also stops receiving security patches. UN R155 requires carmakers to manage cybersecurity across a vehicle's life,13 but owners of unsupported vehicles may not know when that coverage ends. Our audit could flag vehicles past end of support so fleets can decide whether to keep them connected.
Privacy
W1 uses VINs and public specification data. A fleet's vehicle list can reveal operational information, so we would collect only what the audit requires, not share it, and delete it on request. W2 would involve privately owned vehicles and would require clear owner consent before any vehicle data is read. Gap: we have not yet written a data handling policy.
Fairness
We expect that later owners of a vehicle are more likely to be buyers with smaller budgets, and they would bear the cost of lost features without advance notice. This is our assumption and is not yet sourced. If it holds, W2 could help these buyers make informed decisions.
Environment
Vehicles replaced early because their software or connectivity has ended become waste sooner than their mechanical condition requires. Better planning could favor retrofits over replacement where that is practical. We have not quantified this effect.
Data we use
This portfolio uses only public information. No OEM-provided or OEM-sensitive data appears in it.
